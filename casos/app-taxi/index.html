TAXI APP

How detecting and addressing critical UX flaws prevents wasted money and time.

Design Engineer (UX/UI) · Full redesign · 1 month, from analysis to production · Maritime-port services sector.

Context

A maritime and port services company coordinated taxi transfers for crew members in transit: from airports, facilities, hotels and ports, to hospitals, police stations, management centres, airports and hotels. An internal mobile app supported these services, and the company relied on its records for invoicing. Taxi drivers used the app to log trip details, passengers and schedules, and to justify services for billing.

The brief

Original ticket

The app was already in production, launched a year earlier; our task at the time was to extend its functionality, including the ability to attach documentation via photo capture.

The request was framed as an interface task. During functional analysis, it became clear that no further development would add value while a critical usability problem remained.

Finding

The app, meant for trip management, had been built on a calendar-agenda foundation, so the interface's core entity was the scheduled day, not the trip. Everything was modelled as a calendar navigable by date — as a result: buried information, zero hierarchy, and no reduced friction for someone who just needs to know their next job, right now.

Validation

Short remote interviews with taxi drivers, moderated by the product owner, confirmed the diagnosis: they barely opened the app during the day. Instead, they processed all of the day's trips at once, at night.

Decision

With the wrong model confirmed, any incremental improvement — signatures, photos — would have been wasted effort. A full redesign of the flow was proposed, starting from the trip as the core entity. The idea was escalated to the team and product leads; the business approved the review, the sprint task was blocked, and the app's refactor and redesign were scheduled in its place. This was initially a critical moment — acknowledging a flaw in the legacy design and expanding the original task into a full redesign — though its value was confirmed later on.

Solution

The trip as the central entity, grouped in a continuous list by date. Two main screens: upcoming trips and past trips, plus a process window for viewing, editing and changing trip status.

Outcome

  • —Used at the moment of the trip, not batched at the end of the day
  • —Higher volume of records — and more consistency: real signatures, not reconstructed ones
  • —Faster invoicing, fewer issues from incomplete documentation

A slight decrease in trip incidents was also observed — with no clear causal link to the redesign; noted as an observation, not a claimed result.

Building the app around the wrong entity — day instead of trip — accounts for all of the usage symptoms observed. Fixing that foundation, not the visual finish, is what solved the problem.
